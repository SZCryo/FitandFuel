1. What Lift & Fuel actually is
This paper reframes the project around app functionality rather than implementation. The
goal is to define the product clearly enough that the team can design pages, prioritize
features, and begin building.
Lift & Fuel is a guided fitness planning app for students, beginners, and consistency-focused users who
want one place to manage nutrition and training. The app collects a small set of personal inputs — such
as age, height, weight, activity level, goal, dietary restrictions, and available equipment — then returns
a usable plan: daily calories, macro guidance, a meal plan, and a workout structure. From that point
forward, the app becomes a daily operating system for execution: check today's targets, log meals, start
the workout, mark completion, and review history.
Personalized from the start
The first interaction is not generic browsing. The
app asks for profile data, constraints, and goals
so the first result already feels relevant.
One daily dashboard
Users should not have to bounce between
separate meal and workout tools. The diary acts
as the home screen for the day.
Actionable, not abstract
Every recommendation should be attached to
something the user can do now: eat this meal,
hit this protein target, start this workout.
Progress users can feel
History is not just storage. It should help users
understand adherence, consistency, and
whether they are moving toward their goal.
In one sentence: Lift & Fuel turns motivation into a concrete daily plan, then makes it easy to follow and
review that plan.

2. Problem, opportunity, and product goals
The concept deck already identifies the core pain point: users know they want to get
healthier, but struggle to convert personal data into a clear starting plan.
Problem to solve
bullet
Generic diet advice rarely matches a user's
body, schedule, restrictions, or goal.
bullet
Workout plans are intimidating when users do
not know which split, rep ranges, or exercises
make sense for them.
bullet
Nutrition and workouts are often tracked in
separate places, which makes consistency
harder.
bullet
Beginners especially need an app that tells
them what to do today, not just what fitness
terms mean.
Product goals
bullet
Give every new user a personalized plan within
a short onboarding flow.
bullet
Make the daily experience simple enough to
use in under a minute before a meal or
workout.
bullet
Show progress in a way that reinforces
consistency and builds confidence.
bullet
Create a foundation that can become smarter
over time by learning from diary and workout
history.
Who the first version is for
Beginner lifter
Needs a clear starting point for both eating
and training without manually researching
everything.
Busy student
Needs a plan that fits a changing schedule
and reduces decision fatigue around meals
and workouts.
Consistency seeker
May already know the basics, but wants a
simple app that keeps goals visible and
progress measurable.
Non-goals for MVP
bullet
Not a replacement for medical nutrition advice
or injury rehab planning.
bullet
Not a fully social fitness community in the first
release.
bullet
Not an advanced bodybuilding programming
tool with every possible training methodology
on day one.

3. Functional product architecture
Instead of a technical stack diagram, the product should be understood as a closed-loop
system: inputs become a plan, the plan becomes daily actions, and those actions become
history that can improve future guidance.
Lift & Fuel
Daily coach
Profile & Goals
inputs, goal, constraints
Nutrition Plan
calories, macros, meals
Workout Plan
split, exercises, sets, reps
History & Trends
calendar, summaries, progress
Every module should feed the same daily habit loop.
 Module
 Required behavior
Profile & goals
Collect age, height, weight, activity level, primary goal, dietary restrictions,
and available equipment.
Nutrition plan
Return a calorie target, macro guidance, and a customizable meal plan that
can be swapped or regenerated.
Workout plan
Return a weekly split or daily session structure based on goal and
equipment availability.
Diary & execution
Make today's plan visible: calorie progress, macro progress, meals logged,
workout status, and quick actions.
History & feedback loop
Store what happened each day so the app can show summaries, logged
days, streaks, and later adapt future plans.
Design implication: every major feature should support the same question — What does this user need
to do today to move toward their goal?

4. Core feature set
The source materials naturally group the app into five major experiences. These should
become the product pillars around which screens and user stories are organized.
A. Onboarding & plan generation
Collect user inputs, let the user choose a goal, and generate an initial diet and workout plan with visible
calorie and macro targets.
B. Daily diary
Show today's status at a glance: calories, protein, carbs, fat, meals logged, and workout completion state.
C. Meal planning & customization
Provide a 3-course meal plan, show meal-level and day-level macros, and let users edit, swap, or add meals.
D. Workout planning & logging
Give users a routine they can actually run, then let them log sets, reps, weight, duration, and completion.
E. History & analytics
Use a calendar and daily detail views so users can review previous days, logged activity, adherence, and
improvement over time.
These pillars map directly to the concept deck and should also map directly to navigation, engineering
epics, and design workstreams.

10. Functional rules and product logic
The first version does not need an advanced recommendation engine, but it does need clear, predictable
product rules so recommendations feel trustworthy.
 Rule area
 Required product behavior
Profile completeness
Required fields should include age, height, weight, activity level, goal, and at least
one usable diet/equipment preference set before generation.
Goal modes
Initial goal modes should be limited and explicit: lose weight, maintain weight,
build muscle, improve consistency.
Calorie target
The planner should calculate a target from user inputs, then adjust based on goal
mode. The exact formula is less important than consistency and explainability.
Macro guidance
Protein, carbs, fat, and total calories should always be visible at both summary and
diary levels.
Meal planning
The daily meal plan should contain at least breakfast, lunch, and dinner, with
optional snacks or manual additions.
Meal customization
Edit, swap, or regenerate actions must preserve a clear view of what changed and
how it affects totals.
Workout generation
Workout structure should account for goal and equipment, then return exercises
with sets and reps that are beginner-friendly.
Workout logging
Each exercise should support logging actual performance, not only marking the
whole workout complete.
History capture
Every nutrition and workout action should create a day-level record that can be
surfaced later in history.
Future refinement
Prior analytics can later influence next-plan suggestions, but the MVP can start
with rules and stored history before becoming adaptive.

This is the product-data view, not the technical schema. It defines the objects the experience

 Product object
 Why it matters
User profile
Height, weight, age, activity level, goal, restrictions, equipment, units, and optional

calendar day.

12. MVP scope, release sequencing, and
expansion
The MVP should prove that users can go from setup to daily use in one coherent loop.
Everything else should support that loop rather than distract from it.
 Release stage
 Recommended scope
MVP
Onboarding, goal selection, calorie/macro targets, 3-course meal plan, meal edit/swap,
diary dashboard, workout plan, set/rep logging, workout summary, history calendar, day
details.
V1.1
Weekly check-ins, saved meals, streaks, better macro explanations, reminders, richer
history charts, easier plan regeneration.
V2+
Adaptive recommendations from history, grocery list support, coach mode, social
accountability, wearable integrations, habit nudges, deeper analytics.
Suggested product build order
1. Setup first
Build profile intake, goal selection, and plan
generation before polishing secondary screens.
2. Diary shell
Create the daily dashboard early so every new
feature has a clear home.
3. Nutrition loop
Complete meal plan, meal editing, and diary
nutrition tracking as the first end-to-end flow.
4. Workout loop
Add workout plan, active logging, and session
summary as the second end-to-end flow.
5. History loop
Add calendar and daily review only after the app
is already producing real daily records.
6. Smarter iteration
Use real usage patterns to decide what should
adapt, automate, or personalize next.

13. Product success metrics, risks, and closing
recommendation
A strong first version is not the one with the most screens. It is the one that gives users a clear
daily plan and makes them want to come back tomorrow.
Success metrics
bullet
Onboarding completion rate
bullet
Plan generation completion rate
bullet
7-day and 30-day retention
bullet
Diary opens per active user
bullet
Workouts completed per week
bullet
Meal logging frequency
bullet
Percent of days with full nutrition +
workout data
Key product risks
bullet
Too much manual logging creates friction.
bullet
Meal planning may feel rigid if swaps are
weak.
bullet
Workout plans may feel generic if
equipment handling is poor.
bullet
History may feel passive unless it turns data
into encouragement.
bullet
Too many early features could slow the
core diary habit loop.
Closing recommendation
Lift & Fuel should be built as a decision-reducing daily companion, not just a calculator. The app
becomes compelling when the user can open it and immediately know: what to eat, what to train, what
they have already completed, and how they have been doing lately. That product shape is already
visible in the team deck; the next step is to turn these sections directly into tickets, flows, and UI states.
